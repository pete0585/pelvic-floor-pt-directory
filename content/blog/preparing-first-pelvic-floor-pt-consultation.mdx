## Why Preparation Matters

A first pelvic floor physical therapy (PT) consultation is a conversation and an assessment. It is not a test you need to pass, and you do not need to arrive with a diagnosis or a perfect summary of your history. Preparation helps you use the time well, ask the questions that matter to you, and leave with a clearer sense of possible next steps.

This guide offers a practical checklist and a set of questions you can bring to a qualified professional. It is not a clinical protocol, and it does not replace individualized advice. Your situation is specific to you, and a qualified professional can help you understand what may be appropriate in your case.

## Before the Appointment: A Preparation Checklist

Use this checklist as a starting point. You do not need to complete every item, and you can adapt it to your needs.

- **Write down your main concerns.** In one or two sentences, note what prompted you to seek a consultation. For example, you might describe symptoms, changes you have noticed, or goals you want to discuss.
- **Note the timeline.** When did you first notice the issue? Has it changed over time? Are there times when it is better or worse?
- **List what you have already tried.** This could include lifestyle adjustments, exercises, other healthcare visits, or simply waiting to see if things changed. Include what helped, what did not, and what you are unsure about.
- **Bring a current medication and supplement list.** Include names, doses, and how often you take them. If you use any devices or products related to pelvic health, note those as well.
- **Prepare questions about suitability.** Ask whether pelvic floor PT is appropriate for your situation, what alternatives exist, and what the evidence says about different approaches.
- **Ask about the process.** What does an initial evaluation typically involve? What might be discussed or assessed? What can you expect in follow-up visits?
- **Discuss risks and benefits as questions.** Ask what potential risks or downsides might be associated with any recommended approach, and what might happen if you choose to wait or try something else.
- **Clarify logistics.** Ask about appointment length, frequency, location, and what happens if you need to reschedule. If cost is a concern, ask how billing and payment questions are handled before you commit.
- **Plan for comfort.** If you have concerns about privacy, physical comfort, or communication style, you can raise them in advance. You are allowed to ask for accommodations or to pause at any point.
- **Bring a support person if you wish.** Some people find it helpful to have someone with them to take notes or offer support. Ask the provider's office whether this is possible.
- **Take notes.** Bring a notebook or use your phone to record key points. You can also ask whether it is okay to record the conversation, if that helps you remember details.

## Questions to Discuss with a Qualified Professional

The following questions are designed to open a conversation. They are not a script, and you do not need to ask all of them. Choose the ones that feel most relevant to you.

### About Suitability and Goals

- Is pelvic floor PT appropriate for what I am experiencing?
- What are the goals of an initial evaluation, and how would we know if we are making progress?
- Are there other types of care or providers I should consider, either instead of or alongside pelvic floor PT?

### About the Evaluation Process

- What does a typical first visit involve?
- What kinds of questions will you ask me, and what will you be looking for?
- Will there be any physical assessment? If so, what does that involve, and how do you approach comfort and consent?
- What should I expect in terms of follow-up visits, and how often might we meet?

### About Evidence and Alternatives

- What does the current evidence suggest about the approaches you might recommend?
- What are the potential risks or downsides of those approaches?
- What alternatives exist, and what might happen if I choose to wait or try something else first?

### About Communication and Comfort

- How do you prefer to communicate during visits, and how can I let you know if something feels uncomfortable?
- What is your approach to privacy and confidentiality?
- If I have questions between visits, how should I reach you?

### About Logistics and Practicalities

- How long are appointments typically, and how many visits might be involved?
- What is your policy on cancellations and rescheduling?
- How are billing and payment questions handled? (Note: this guide does not cover pricing details; ask the provider's office directly for their policies.)

## Making the Most of the Conversation

A first consultation is an opportunity to gather information and decide whether the provider and approach feel like a good fit for you. You are not obligated to continue with any provider if the fit does not feel right. It is reasonable to ask for clarification, to take time to think, or to seek a second opinion.

If you are unsure where to start, you can review general guidance on choosing a doctor or healthcare service from [MedlinePlus](https://medlineplus.gov/choosingadoctororhealthcareservice.html). That resource offers a broad overview of factors to consider when selecting a healthcare provider.

## After the Appointment

After your consultation, you may want to take a few minutes to note your impressions. Did you feel heard? Were your questions answered in a way you could understand? Do you have a clear sense of possible next steps and how to decide among them? These reflections can help you decide whether to continue with that provider or to explore other options.

If you decide to look at other providers, you can use a similar checklist and set of questions. Consistency in how you evaluate options can help you compare them more clearly. You might also consider what matters most to you, such as communication style, appointment availability, or the range of services offered.

## Final Thoughts

Preparing for a first pelvic floor PT consultation does not require special expertise. It requires a bit of reflection, a list of questions, and a willingness to have an open conversation. Bring your concerns, ask about suitability, evidence, risks, and alternatives, and take notes. You are in charge of your care decisions, and a qualified professional can help you understand your options. If you are unsure about anything, ask. A good consultation is a dialogue, and your questions are a valuable part of it.
